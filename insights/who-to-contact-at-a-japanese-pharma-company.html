Who Should Overseas Biotech Companies Contact at a Japanese Pharma Company?

Identifying potential pharmaceutical partners in Japan is relatively straightforward. The more difficult task is finding the people inside each company who can understand, evaluate, and advance your proposal.

Akiko Yoshida, MSc · September 21, 2026

Business Development may seem like the obvious place to start. However, the appropriate contact depends on what you are offering, the maturity of your technology or business, and what you want the Japanese company to do next.

Based on my recent experience supporting outreach and collaboration discussions between overseas life science companies and potential partners in Japan, I have found that selecting the right company is only the first step. Effective outreach also requires an understanding of the different entry points and stakeholders within that company.

This is a practical starting framework, not a universal organizational map. Department names, responsibilities, and decision-making processes vary considerably among pharmaceutical companies.

1. Business Development and Licensing

Business Development or Licensing can be a natural starting point when your company is offering:

  • A drug candidate or development-stage asset
  • An in-licensing or out-licensing opportunity
  • Development or commercialization rights in a particular region
  • A co-development opportunity
  • A technology platform linked to a specific therapeutic program

These teams generally assess whether an opportunity is commercially and strategically relevant to the company. They may also coordinate internal reviews involving Research, Clinical Development, Legal, Intellectual Property, and Commercial teams.

However, a general platform introduction without a clear asset, use case, or partnership model may not immediately fit their responsibilities.

Instead of simply stating, “We have this technology,” explain the problem it addresses, who would use it, and what type of transaction or collaboration you are proposing.

2. Open Innovation and External Innovation

Some pharmaceutical companies have Open Innovation, External Innovation, or Strategic Partnership functions responsible for identifying technologies and collaboration opportunities outside the organization.

These teams can be useful entry points for:

  • Early-stage technologies
  • Academic discoveries
  • Drug discovery platforms
  • Startup partnerships
  • Joint research proposals
  • Opportunities that do not yet fit a conventional licensing process

Because these teams may work across multiple internal functions, they can sometimes connect an overseas company with the most relevant scientific or business team.

However, “open innovation” can mean different things at different companies. One team may focus on sourcing research assets, while another may be responsible for startup engagement, corporate venture investment, academic collaboration, or broader ecosystem development.

Before making contact, check what the company is currently seeking and whether its priority therapeutic areas and technology interests align with your proposal.

3. Research and Drug Discovery

Research and Drug Discovery teams may play an important role in evaluating technologies such as:

  • AI-enabled drug discovery
  • Virtual screening and molecular modeling
  • Target identification and validation
  • Biomarker discovery
  • Preclinical research tools
  • Technologies addressing a specific drug discovery challenge

Scientists can assess whether a technology is technically relevant to an existing research program. If it addresses a real and timely problem, they may also become internal advocates for the opportunity.

However, Research teams do not necessarily accept direct external inquiries. In some companies, proposals are first submitted through an official partnering channel or Open Innovation team and are then shared with the relevant scientists.

Research teams may also lack the budget or authority required to establish an external partnership. Technical interest is therefore important, but it may not be sufficient to move a project forward.

4. Corporate Strategy and Portfolio Strategy

Corporate Strategy and Portfolio Strategy teams are not necessarily the first point of contact for external proposals. However, they may become involved during the evaluation and decision-making process when an opportunity concerns:

  • Therapeutic-area priorities
  • Portfolio-level decisions
  • Market and competitive intelligence
  • Technology landscape analysis
  • Strategic partnerships affecting multiple business units
  • Decisions about where the company should invest or build future capabilities

These teams consider how an opportunity fits the company’s overall direction rather than assessing only one experiment or technology.

For an overseas startup, this means that explaining the technical capabilities alone may not be enough. The proposal should also communicate why the opportunity matters strategically, what decision or priority it supports, and how it complements the company’s existing portfolio or internal capabilities.

5. Digital Transformation and Data Science

For technologies involving data infrastructure, analytics, workflow improvement, or enterprise AI, Digital Transformation, Data Science, IT, or a relevant business function may become involved in the evaluation.

Examples include:

  • Research data platforms
  • Clinical or real-world data analytics
  • Knowledge management tools
  • AI-enabled internal workflows
  • Data integration and visualization
  • Enterprise software for pharmaceutical operations

It is important to distinguish between an AI technology used directly in drug discovery and an enterprise tool designed to improve internal operations.

Although both may be described as “AI,” they can have very different users, budgets, evaluation criteria, and implementation processes. Enterprise proposals may also require input from Information Security, Procurement, Legal, Compliance, and teams responsible for integration with existing systems.

Not Every Relevant Function Is a Direct Entry Point

Not all of the functions described above accept direct external inquiries.

Depending on the company, the most appropriate entry point may be:

  • An official partnering or licensing channel
  • An Open Innovation program
  • A technology call or joint research program
  • A conference partnering system
  • An introduction through an existing contact or industry specialist

Research, Corporate Strategy, and Data Science teams may become involved later in the technical evaluation or decision-making process, even when they are not the initial point of contact.

Rather than relying only on an organizational chart to identify individuals, check how each pharmaceutical company formally receives and reviews external opportunities.

One Company May Involve Multiple Stakeholders

Finding one relevant contact does not necessarily mean that you have reached everyone required to advance the opportunity.

A potential collaboration may involve at least three different roles:

  1. The technical evaluator, who determines whether the technology works and is scientifically relevant
  2. The budget owner, who can allocate funding for a pilot or collaboration
  3. The internal champion, who is willing to coordinate the discussion and move the opportunity through the organization

One person may perform more than one of these roles. In many cases, however, they belong to different departments.

This is one reason why initial interest does not always lead directly to a pilot project. The person who understands the technology may not control the budget, while the person responsible for partnerships may need support from a Research or business team.

Adapt the Message to the Recipient

Sending the same introduction to every function is unlikely to be effective.

  • Research may focus on scientific methodology, validation data, and relevance to a specific program.
  • Business Development may focus on the asset, development stage, intellectual property, and proposed deal structure.
  • Strategy may focus on how the opportunity fits the company’s portfolio and long-term priorities.
  • Digital or IT may evaluate system integration, security, implementation requirements, and organizational use.

Before contacting someone, an overseas biotech company should be able to answer:

  • What exactly are we proposing?
  • Why is it relevant to this company?
  • Which internal team is most likely to experience the problem we solve?
  • What is the smallest reasonable next step?
  • Who would need to evaluate, fund, and support that step?

Do Not Share Too Much Confidential Information Initially

An initial approach should provide enough information for the recipient to understand the technology and assess its potential relevance. At the same time, companies should avoid disclosing unnecessary confidential information before a nondisclosure agreement is in place.

An initial non-confidential introduction might include:

  • An overview of the technology or asset
  • The problem it addresses
  • The relevant therapeutic area or use case
  • Its current stage of development or validation
  • Results that can be shared publicly
  • The proposed form of collaboration
  • A suggested next step

Before sharing unpublished chemical structures, detailed research data, proprietary algorithms, or information governed by agreements with third parties, confirm the confidentiality status and the conditions under which the information can be disclosed.

When using an official partnering portal, review its terms regarding submitted information and confidential materials before uploading anything.

Move from Company Lists to Stakeholder Mapping

A list of Japanese pharmaceutical companies can help identify potential targets, but it is not yet a Japan market-entry strategy.

The next layer is stakeholder mapping: understanding each company’s research priorities, relevant functions, potential internal users, decision-makers, and formal channels for receiving external proposals.

The objective is not to find one universally correct job title. It is to develop a hypothesis about where your opportunity fits within the organization and then refine that hypothesis through conversations.

For an overseas biotech company approaching Japan, the question is not only:

“Which pharmaceutical companies should we contact?”

It is also:

“Who inside each company could understand, evaluate, fund, and advance this particular opportunity?”

And finally:

“Which entry point should we use to reach those people?”

Considering all three questions can make outreach more focused and increase the likelihood that an initial introduction develops into a meaningful collaboration.
