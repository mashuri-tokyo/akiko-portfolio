How Do Overseas Medicines and Digital Health Technologies Reach Japan?

Three diabetes partnerships and what they tell us about entering the Japanese market.

Akiko Yoshida, MSc · September 14, 2026

When international life science companies consider entering Japan, what role can partnerships with pharmaceutical companies play?

Examples from diabetes care show several forms of collaboration: licensing drug candidates, jointly developing digital therapeutics, and connecting digital services with existing treatments.

This article looks at three partnerships: Astellas and Welldoc, Sumitomo Pharma and Poxel, and Sanofi and Health2Sync. Drawing on official company announcements, it examines what each company brought to the partnership and how their roles were defined.

About this review. This article is based on official announcements published between 2017 and 2024, together with product integration documentation. It does not provide a complete account of the latest status of each partnership or product. Development plans are distinguished from confirmed progress in sales and service delivery.

1. Astellas × Welldoc: Jointly Developing Digital Therapeutics for Japan

In November 2019, Astellas announced a strategic partnership with US-based Welldoc. The partnership covered BlueStar, a digital health product that supports diabetes self-management.

Under the agreement, the companies would jointly develop and commercialize BlueStar in Japan and certain other Asian markets. They would also work together to expand access in the United States. The agreement included a US$15 million upfront payment from Astellas, with potential milestone payments linked to development and commercialization, as well as royalties on future product sales.[1]

Both companies explained their goals. Welldoc saw opportunities to expand into other diseases and global markets. Astellas positioned the partnership within its strategy to develop its Rx+ business.[1]

In 2023, Astellas announced a separate agreement with Roche Diabetes Care Japan. This agreement covered the development and commercialization in Japan of a combined medical product pairing BlueStar with the Accu-Chek Guide Me blood glucose monitoring system.

The announcement stated that Astellas would be responsible for clinical trials of the combined product and would seek regulatory approval and reimbursement. At that point, the trials were planned; the announcement did not report approval or launch in Japan.[2]

This case illustrates how bringing an overseas digital product to Japan can involve connecting an app, a medical device, and clinical development capabilities. A partner’s role can extend well beyond sales.

2. Sumitomo Pharma × Poxel: Combining Development and Commercialization Rights with Investment

In October 2017, Sumitomo Dainippon Pharma, now Sumitomo Pharma, announced a development and commercialization partnership with France-based Poxel.

The agreement covered imeglimin, then an investigational treatment for type 2 diabetes. Sumitomo Dainippon Pharma acquired development and commercialization rights and other rights in Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, and nine Southeast Asian countries.

The companies planned to conduct Phase III trials jointly in Japan, with Sumitomo Dainippon Pharma covering the trial costs. The agreement included an upfront payment of ¥4.75 billion, development and sales milestone payments, and royalties.[3]

The stage of development at the time of the agreement is worth examining. Imeglimin had already undergone Phase I and II studies in the United States, Europe, and Japan. The official announcement also described Poxel’s involvement in joint development and its support for the Japanese application for marketing approval.[3]

Imeglimin was subsequently launched in Japan as TWYMEEG in September 2021. In August 2024, the two companies jointly announced results from a post-marketing clinical study in Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes and renal impairment.[4]

This case provides a record of progress from a partnership agreement to sales in Japan, followed by the announcement of additional clinical data.

3. Sanofi × Health2Sync: Connecting Existing Treatments with Digital Services

In June 2022, Sanofi’s Japanese subsidiary and H2, Health2Sync’s Japanese subsidiary, announced a partnership concerning a digital support program for diabetes care.

At the time of the announcement, H2’s health and lifestyle support app had approximately 290,000 users in Japan. Sanofi and Health2Sync had been collaborating in Taiwan since September 2020, and the Japanese partnership extended that collaboration into another market.

The 2022 announcement outlined plans to develop digital tools using connected caps for insulin pens and insulin dose adjustment alerts, intended to support healthcare professionals and patients receiving insulin therapy.[5]

Subsequent SoloSmart integration instructions describe how the device records insulin injection times and doses and transfers those records to the Health2Sync app via Bluetooth.

However, the same documentation states that SoloSmart is a non-medical device in Japan and that the Health2Sync app itself was not jointly developed with Sanofi.[6]

Connecting products through a partnership does not mean that the companies jointly developed every product or share all responsibilities. The materials reviewed for this article also do not establish whether the dose adjustment alerts described in the 2022 announcement were implemented in Japan.

This case can be understood as an example of a company with an existing Japanese user base seeking to expand its support for diabetes care through collaboration with a pharmaceutical company.

Across these three cases, companies combined technologies or drug assets with capabilities needed to develop and expand their use in Japan.

However, licensing, joint development, and product integration involve different agreements and divisions of responsibility. Describing all three as “buying an overseas product and having the Japanese partner take over all clinical trials and regulatory work” would miss these differences.

What Should Companies Clarify Before Looking for a Partner in Japan?

The following observations are my interpretation of these three cases.

Before identifying potential Japanese partners, international companies should consider their current stage of development and the capabilities they need to complement their own.

A company with clinical data for a drug candidate may need a partner for further development, investment, and commercialization in Japan. A digital health company may need support with device integration or clinical evaluation. For a service that already has Japanese users, connecting more closely with existing treatments could be the next objective.

It is also useful to explain why the potential partner would want to collaborate.

In the Poxel partnership, Sumitomo sought to strengthen its diabetes product portfolio. In the Welldoc partnership, Astellas identified a connection with its Rx+ strategy.[1][3]

When considering a potential partner, company size is only one factor. Companies can also examine how their technology relates to a particular business or problem within the partner organization. That hypothesis can help define the questions to explore in an initial meeting.

When planning an approach to Japan, I believe it is useful to consider the following question alongside a list of potential partners:

What capabilities do we need to bring our technology to Japan, and with whom should we share which responsibilities?

Making this question more specific can help companies assess potential opportunities for collaboration.
