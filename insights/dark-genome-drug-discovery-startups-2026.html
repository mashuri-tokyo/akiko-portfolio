Beyond the 2%: Why Biotech Startups Are Mining the Dark Genome

Ancient virus-derived sequences once dismissed as genomic debris are emerging as potential therapeutic targets in cancer, autoimmune disease, and neurodegeneration. A small group of startups is now turning what was written off as junk DNA into a drug-discovery frontier.

Akiko Yoshida, MSc · August 31, 2026

Drug discovery has long focused on the roughly 2% of the human genome that encodes proteins — the exome. By contrast, regions rich in repetitive sequences have been difficult to analyze, and their biological functions and relevance to drug discovery have received far less attention.

That view is beginning to change.

Startups Covered in This Guide

Several biotech startups are now searching the so-called dark genome for new drug targets, tumor antigens, and biomarkers. Following the usage in the original article this guide draws from, "dark genome" here refers to repeat-rich and ancient virus-like sequences found largely outside conventional protein-coding genes. Two prominent examples are LINE-1 (long interspersed nuclear element-1) retrotransposons and human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs).

The biology of LINE-1 and HERVs and their possible links to disease have been studied by universities and research institutions for years. Startups are now attempting to translate that knowledge into reverse transcriptase inhibitors, antibodies, cancer vaccines, and other therapies.

Ancient Virus-Derived Sequences Hidden in Our DNA

LINE-1 has existed in the genome for more than 100 million years. Most copies are inactive fragments, but approximately 150 intact sequences may retain the ability to proliferate.

An active LINE-1 element is first transcribed into RNA. A reverse transcriptase encoded by LINE-1 then converts that RNA back into DNA, enabling LINE-1 to proliferate through a "copy-and-paste" mechanism within the genome.

HERVs have a different origin. They are remnants of ancient retroviruses that entered the genomes of our ancestors. Some retain protein-coding genes resembling those of retroviruses such as HIV, but they have lost the ability to produce replication-competent infectious viral particles. Certain elements can nevertheless still express viral proteins, including the envelope protein Env.

Cells normally silence many LINE-1 and HERV sequences through DNA methylation, heterochromatin, and other epigenetic controls. Aging, infection, cellular stress, malignant transformation, and changes associated with neurodegeneration can sometimes weaken this repression.

When these sequences are reactivated, they may produce pathological effects.

Reactivated HERVs and retrotransposons can generate RNA that is reverse-transcribed back into DNA. DNA accumulating in the cytosol can set off an alarm resembling a viral infection, potentially triggering interferon responses and chronic inflammation. The precise mechanisms and causal relationships, however, remain unresolved and may differ across diseases.

But in cancer, the same reactivation may carry the opposite meaning.

HERV-derived proteins expressed in malignant cells could help distinguish tumors from normal tissue, potentially creating new targets for vaccines and T-cell therapies. The therapeutic strategies pursued by startups therefore divide into two apparently opposite directions: silencing dark-genome activity that may drive inflammation, or exploiting dark-genome expression that exposes cancer cells to the immune system.

Strategy 1: Suppressing LINE-1-Associated Inflammation

Rome Therapeutics and Transposon Therapeutics have worked on nucleoside analogs intended to inhibit LINE-1 reverse transcriptase. The idea resembles antiretroviral treatment for HIV, but the molecular targets are not identical.

Structural research has identified important differences between HIV reverse transcriptase and LINE-1 reverse transcriptase. Many existing HIV drugs are therefore unlikely to be optimal LINE-1 inhibitors — a distinction that creates room to select or design compounds specifically for dark-genome targets.

Transposon Therapeutics

Transposon's lead candidate, TPN-101, was originally developed as an HIV reverse transcriptase inhibitor. It is reported to be one of relatively few compounds that also inhibit LINE-1 reverse transcriptase. The program has a notable connection to Japan: TPN-101 was licensed from the Japanese biotech company Oncolys BioPharma.

Transposon has evaluated TPN-101 in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), a neurodegenerative disorder associated with abnormal tau accumulation. One hypothesis is that tau-related disruption of heterochromatin exposes LINE-1 elements that would normally remain silent, and that reactivated LINE-1 may then provoke damaging inflammation.

According to the company, a small Phase 2a study involving 42 participants found that the clinical rating score in the highest-dose 400 mg group remained near baseline after 24 weeks, while some biomarkers associated with neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation declined. This was a small, exploratory study divided into four treatment groups, and the results do not by themselves establish clinical efficacy in slowing disease progression.

The company has also evaluated TPN-101 in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Interim Phase 2 findings reportedly showed a possible signal that LINE-1 inhibition could help preserve respiratory function — an interim company report, with clinical efficacy not yet established.

Rome Therapeutics

Rome Therapeutics has been developing a novel nucleoside analog against LINE-1 reverse transcriptase as a more target-specific approach, with initial interest in autoimmune diseases driven by interferon signaling.

In preclinical research presented at a scientific meeting, the candidate RPT-A reportedly reduced LINE-1-associated inflammatory markers in a mouse model of Aicardi–Goutières syndrome. Abnormal LINE-1 activation was also reported in skin biopsies from people with lupus. These findings support the therapeutic hypothesis, but they do not demonstrate safety or efficacy in humans.

If LINE-1 activity lies upstream of inflammatory signaling, could autoimmune disease be treated without broadly suppressing the immune system?

That possibility remains unproven clinically. But it illustrates why the dark genome has attracted attention: the approach could reveal mechanisms closer to the origin of disease than conventional downstream inflammatory targets.

Strategy 2: Turning HERVs Into Cancer Targets

In cancer, reactivated HERVs may be more than harmful by-products — they might also be therapeutically exploitable. Normal gene regulation breaks down during malignant transformation, and sequences that are silent in healthy adult tissues may consequently be expressed in tumors, including HERV-derived proteins. If peptides from those proteins are presented to the immune system, cancer cells may display virus-like markers.

Correction to the record. A 2023 study reporting an association between antibodies against HERV-derived proteins and clinical outcomes in lung cancer was retracted in January 2026, after an institutional investigation identified manipulated data and problems with reproducibility and data integrity. Its results can no longer be treated as evidence for the clinical utility of HERV-derived antigens. The broader hypothesis continues to be investigated, but individual candidates require independent validation.

Evaxion Biotech

Evaxion uses computational methods to identify antigens predicted to elicit effective T-cell responses, and has added a HERV-focused algorithm called OBSERV to its discovery workflow. This may be particularly relevant to cancers with a low tumor mutational burden: when few useful mutation-derived neoantigens are available, HERV-derived antigens could provide another source of potentially tumor-specific targets.

Evaxion has reported preclinical data indicating that HERV antigens can induce antitumor responses, exploring their use in both personalized and off-the-shelf vaccines. Efficacy of a therapeutic vaccine incorporating HERV antigens has not yet been demonstrated in humans.

Hervolution Therapeutics

Hervolution has been developing a vaccine targeting the HERV-K Env protein, aiming to induce two complementary immune responses: antiviral antibodies and CD8 killer T cells. The company has explored both adenoviral-vector and mRNA vaccine formats, reporting strong antitumor activity in animal models when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

In a related approach, Enara Bio has searched the dark genome for previously overlooked antigens that could serve as targets for cancer immunotherapy.

HERVs in Neurological and Post-Infectious Disease

HERV-derived proteins have also been studied outside cancer. GeNeuro has been developing monoclonal antibodies against HERV Env proteins — in ALS, an antibody targeting HERV-K Env, which had been linked to neurological damage.

The company's experience also illustrates the field's uncertainty. Despite evidence linking HERV-W reactivation to multiple sclerosis (MS), the HERV-W Env-targeting antibody temelimab failed to prevent new lesions in a Phase 2 trial. GeNeuro subsequently advanced a Phase 2 study of temelimab in Long COVID, based on observations that HERV-W protein could be detected in a subset of patients — a case that illustrates the importance of identifying patients in whom the relevant HERV target is active.

Could the Dark Genome Also Contribute to Aging?

The field has begun to extend into aging research. A 2023 study suggested that HERV-K reactivation may contribute to cellular senescence and age-related inflammation. Researchers from Altos Labs participated in the study, and Altos has filed a patent covering antisense oligonucleotides intended to inactivate LINE-1 in age-related diseases. Neither participation in a study nor a patent application demonstrates therapeutic benefit in humans.

This remains a highly exploratory application. Even if repeat-element activation is observed during aging, it has not been shown that inhibiting these elements can extend healthspan or treat age-related disease. The hypothesis nevertheless illustrates how broadly the mechanism might apply if a causal relationship can be established.

Remaining Challenges

The field still faces major uncertainties. Despite substantial evidence associating HERV-W reactivation with MS, temelimab did not prevent new lesions in a Phase 2 trial.

There are also distinctive translational challenges. The human and mouse "repeatomes" — the complete sets of repetitive sequences — differ substantially, making it difficult to reproduce human-specific mechanisms in animal models. Omics protocols and analysis pipelines may need to be redesigned to capture repetitive sequences accurately. Researchers must also determine which patients have activation of the relevant LINE-1 or HERV element, and develop biomarkers suitable for use in clinical trials.

Much remains unknown about dark-genome structure and function — but the size of this unknown territory may itself create drug-discovery opportunities.

An Emerging Field Led by Startups

Companies including Rome Therapeutics, Transposon Therapeutics, Evaxion Biotech, Hervolution Therapeutics, Enara Bio, and GeNeuro have pursued reverse transcriptase inhibitors, antibodies, and therapeutic vaccines directed at repetitive sequences that historically received little attention.

The field remains small and is driven mainly by startups rather than large pharmaceutical companies. The Dark Genome Symposium, launched by Rome Therapeutics in 2022, has developed into an annual forum where startups, clinicians, and researchers exchange insights. Enara Bio's Joseph Dukes has summarized the field's attitude as collaborating where possible and competing where necessary.

Alongside early clinical signals, the field has also seen unsuccessful trials and continuing challenges involving animal models and analytical methods. Opening up this large and poorly understood territory will require persistent research.

What's Changed Since 2024

The reporting above draws on a Nature Biotechnology feature published in April 2024. Since then, several of the companies it covered have moved — in both directions. The following is limited to facts confirmed in company announcements, public agency materials, or regulatory disclosures available through August 31, 2026.

Transposon Therapeutics

In May 2025, Transposon announced that TPN-101 had been selected for inclusion in the Phase 2/3 HEALEY ALS Platform Trial. That announcement documented the selection and an anticipated start date; this guide has not confirmed subsequent initiation or results for the TPN-101 regimen.

New: aging research. In February 2026, ARPA-H named the research teams selected for its PROSPR (Proactive Solutions for Prolonging Resilience) program. A team led by the University of Rochester will evaluate TPN-101 as a potential intervention for healthy aging, supported by a milestone-contingent contract of up to $22 million over as many as five years. Transposon participates alongside Brown University, the University of Connecticut, and other institutions — the award is not a $22 million payment to Transposon alone.

Evaxion Biotech

Evaxion is conducting preclinical development of EVX-04, an off-the-shelf vaccine candidate for acute myeloid leukemia that targets multiple ERV antigens from the dark genome. In an August 2026 update, the company said it was preparing a regulatory filing for clinical testing alongside ongoing preclinical work, and expected to submit it before the end of 2026. As of that update, EVX-04 remained preclinical, and safety and efficacy had not been demonstrated in humans.

Enara Bio

In January 2026, Enara Bio announced that Boehringer Ingelheim had exercised an option to license multiple additional Dark Antigen targets discovered and validated through the companies' collaboration.

Enara is developing ENA101, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting DARKFOX, a cancer-specific Dark Antigen encoded by an alternative open reading frame within FOXM1. The company announced in November 2025 that ENA101 was advancing into IND-enabling studies, presented preclinical data at AACR in April 2026, and in its January 2026 update said it planned to advance the program into First-in-Human studies.

GeNeuro

In June 2024, GeNeuro announced results from a Phase 2 trial of temelimab in people with neuropsychiatric symptoms of Long COVID. More than 200 participants who tested positive for HERV-W ENV were enrolled. The study showed no clinically meaningful improvement over placebo on its primary endpoint measuring improvement in fatigue, and the majority of secondary endpoints did not show an effect. The treatment was reported to be safe and well tolerated.

The result establishes that temelimab did not meet the prespecified primary endpoint, even in a population selected for HERV-W ENV positivity. It does not, by itself, determine whether HERV-W ENV plays a causal role in the condition, or explain why the trial was unsuccessful.

Company update: bankruptcy. GeNeuro SA subsequently entered debt-restructuring proceedings. On January 28, 2026, the Geneva Court of First Instance declined to extend the moratorium and declared the company bankrupt. According to the company's disclosure, it had considered recapitalization, advancing or monetizing development assets, and agreements with creditors, but could not finalize a restructuring solution that would ensure long-term viability. The public disclosures do not establish a direct causal relationship between the unsuccessful clinical trial and the bankruptcy.

No update is provided for Rome Therapeutics or Hervolution Therapeutics, as this review did not identify sufficient primary evidence to establish their current clinical status.

Two years on, the dark genome has produced a $22M federal aging award, a licensing deal with Boehringer Ingelheim, a retraction, and a bankruptcy — the field is still very much being written.
